A printed label, a QR code, a hologram, a lot number stencilled on a drum. Most of what a supply chain uses to prove authenticity sits on the outside of the product. Each mark says something about the contents, and each one depends on the packaging staying with the contents it was printed for.

For a sealed retail pack, that usually holds. For an ingredient, it often does not.

## Where the label and the ingredient part ways

Ingredients get decanted, blended, repacked and relabelled on the way from grower to processor to brand. At each of those steps the original packaging is left behind.

The documents move on with the material. The physical link between the paperwork and the ingredient breaks every time the ingredient changes container.

## What an external mark can tell you

A label verifies the label. A scanned code confirms that a genuine code was printed on a package at some point. Neither can say whether the material inside is the material that was originally packed.

That gap is where the familiar problems sit: genuine packaging refilled with something cheaper, a premium ingredient cut with a lower grade, a valid document attached to a different lot. A QR code can be photographed and reprinted. A tamper-evident seal shows that a container was opened, and once the contents are blended into something else it has nothing more to say.

Labels are still useful. They are fast, cheap and understood by everyone who handles the goods, and every supply chain needs them. Their limit is simple. They travel with the packaging, and the packaging is not the product.

## Marking the material itself

Embedded tagging starts at the other end. It marks the ingredient instead of the container.

NaturalTag is itself a food-grade, non-GMO food ingredient. It is formulated into the ingredient at its source and becomes part of the product. When the ingredient is repacked or blended downstream, the tag goes with it, because it is in it.

Checking works on a sample of the product. NaturalDetect looks for the tag in that sample using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a standard laboratory testing method used widely across the food industry. We call this PCR-based verification. The question it answers is direct: did this material come from the tagged source?

## What changes for a buyer

For an ingredient buyer, the difference shows up at the points that are hardest to check today.

- When goods arrive, a sample from the delivered lot can be tested for the tag, so acceptance does not rest on the documents alone.
- After blending, a tag formulated into a branded ingredient can still be looked for in the finished product, once the tag and the test have been confirmed to work in that product type.
- When a batch is questioned, the evidence is batch-level and comes from the material itself.

For a nutraceutical brand owner who pays for a specific, differentiated ingredient, the same check answers a plain question. Is the ingredient I paid for the one that went into my product?

## What it does not do

Embedded tagging verifies identity and origin. It does not measure how much of an ingredient is present, and it does not replace the quality testing a brand already runs.

It also has to be set up product by product. Oils, powders, fresh produce and capsules all behave differently during sample preparation and testing, so every new product starts with a compatibility check before a tag goes into production.

Labels and codes will keep their place in the supply chain. What embedded tagging adds is evidence-based verification carried by the ingredient itself.
